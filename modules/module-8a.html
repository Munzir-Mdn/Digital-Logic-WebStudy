1. Counter Fundamentals

A counter is a sequential circuit that moves through a prescribed sequence of states when input/count pulses are applied. The sequence can be binary or another prescribed sequence. Counters retain state using internal flip-flops; the module lists J-K, T and D flip-flops as common choices.

Main categories: Asynchronous and Synchronous.

2. Counter Characteristics

Clocking

Asynchronous or synchronous.

Direction

UP, DOWN or UP-DOWN.

Modulus

Number of states in the count sequence.

Repeat

Recycle or saturated behavior.

For an n-bit counter, the normal range shown in the module is 0 to 2ⁿ−1. The complement of an UP sequence counts in the reverse direction, and vice versa.

3. Asynchronous / Ripple Counter

The flip-flops do not change at exactly the same time because they do not have a common clock pulse. The external clock is connected only to the LSB flip-flop; later flip-flops receive their clocks from the previous flip-flop.

This stage-to-stage clocking produces propagation delay — the reason the asynchronous counter is also discussed as a ripple counter.

5. Truncated / Decade Counters

The maximum possible number of states is 2ⁿ, but a counter can be designed to recycle earlier. The module calls MOD-10 a common truncated sequence: a decade counter.

MOD-10: decode 10 = 1010₂ with a NAND gate and connect its output to CLEAR.
MOD-12: decode 12 = 1100₂ and connect the decoder output to CLEAR.

The MOD-10 waveform in the slide also points out a glitch in Q1: Q1 must first go HIGH before count 10 can be decoded, then the decoding gate changes after a small delay.

6. Advantage & Disadvantage

Advantage: simple design. Disadvantage: propagation delay accumulates in a ripple counter, which can cause missing count states at high frequency.

The module gives the maximum operating-frequency relation:

fmax = 1 / (N × tpd)
